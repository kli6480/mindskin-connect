Building a Good-Enough Routine

A simplified routine you can actually keep beats a perfect one you abandon.

A 'good enough' routine means having a scaled-back version ready for stressful weeks — not zero care, just less. Three steps instead of seven. One moisturizer instead of three products.
When stress peaks, stopping entirely removes the protection your skin needs most. Keeping even two or three anchor habits going is far better than starting from scratch.
Plan your 'good enough' version in advance. What are the two or three things you will always do, no matter what? Those are your anchor habits — the ones you protect first.

Your Flare Prevention Plan

Acting early — before a flare fully develops — usually means a milder flare.

1
Know your early warning signs

The signs specific to you that a flare is starting — increased itch at a usual spot, redness at the elbow creases, skin feeling tight. Write these down and keep them in your Eczema Action Plan.

2
Have a step-up plan ready

Know in advance what you'll do when those signs appear. Acting before a flare takes hold takes far less effort than treating a full flare.

3
Check in after stressful periods

A flare often appears one to three days after a high-stress period. Increase moisturizing proactively after a tough week — don't wait for symptoms to appear.

Calming Techniques Under Five Minutes

Mindfulness means paying attention to the present moment without judgment. Even five minutes a day can reduce stress hormones and scratch urges.

1
Mindful Breathing (1–5 min)

Focus on your breath. Notice the air entering and leaving. When your mind wanders, gently bring it back — no judgment. This lowers cortisol and helps interrupt the itch-tension cycle.

2
Quick Body Scan (2–5 min)

Mentally scan from toes to head, noticing any tension without judgment. Consciously release it as you go. Works especially well before sleep when itch often peaks.

3
5-4-3-2-1 Grounding (1–3 min)

Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste or one positive thought. Brings you back to the present when stress or itch escalates.

PMR and CBT — Two Tools Worth Knowing

Evidence-based techniques that directly reduce itch, anxiety, and sleep problems.

1
Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)

Systematically tense and release muscle groups from feet to face. This can lower stress and help you sleep. Use our PMR guide (linked from this module) to try it tonight.

2
Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

CBT has been shown to reduce itch intensity, sleep problems, anxiety, and depression in people with eczema. It works by changing the thought patterns and behaviours that make symptoms worse.

3
Accessing CBT in Alberta

MindShift CBT (free app), BounceBack Alberta (free telephone coaching program), or ask your doctor for a referral to a therapist who works with chronic conditions.

Thinking it Through

Question 1 of 4
It's been a brutal week at work — you're exhausted, stressed, and your usual 7-step skincare routine feels completely overwhelming. According to what we know about routines and eczema:
A 'good enough' routine is one you actually do. During high-stress periods, your skin needs protection most — but so does your mental energy. Deciding in advance what your minimum anchor habits are means you never have to choose between all-or-nothing.

Food and Your Skin

What you eat doesn't cause eczema — but it can influence inflammation.

A skin-friendly diet is an anti-inflammatory diet: more fruits, vegetables, and whole grains; less processed food and added sugar.
No single food causes eczema. The idea that one food is 'the culprit' is rarely supported by evidence — and removing entire food groups without guidance can cause nutritional harm.
Talk to your doctor before cutting out any food group. If a food allergy is contributing to your eczema, a proper allergy test or food challenge is the right way to find it.

What the Evidence Actually Shows

Some useful food guidance — without the myths.

Omega-3s

Fatty fish, flaxseed, and walnuts have anti-inflammatory properties. Some people with eczema find these helpful as part of a balanced diet.

Probiotics

Foods like yogurt and fermented vegetables may support gut health, which is linked to skin health. The evidence is still emerging — they won't hurt.

Hydration

Well-hydrated skin is more resilient. Aim for 6–8 glasses of water per day — and more during flares when your skin barrier is most compromised.

Food Allergies

If you suspect a food trigger, talk to your doctor about a proper evaluation. A supervised food challenge or allergy test gives you real, actionable information.

Removing foods without medical guidance can lead to nutritional deficiencies and an unnecessarily restricted diet. Your doctor or a registered dietitian is your best guide.

Thinking it Through

Question 2 of 4
A friend tells you to cut out dairy, gluten, and all added sugar because they read it cures eczema. Based on what this module covers:
Right. Food relationships with eczema are complex. A supervised food challenge or allergy test is the appropriate way to identify a food trigger — not an unguided elimination diet that may cause nutritional deficiency or a difficult long-term relationship with food.

Moving Your Body, Protecting Your Skin

Regular moderate exercise lowers cortisol, improves sleep, and supports mood.

1
During flares: choose lower-sweat activities

Swimming, gentle yoga, walking, and stretching reduce heat and sweat — both of which can irritate inflamed skin and intensify itch.

2
After exercise: the 3-minute window

Shower with lukewarm (not hot) water as soon as possible. Pat dry gently and apply moisturizer within 3 minutes — the same Soak and Seal principle from Module 2.

3
Dress and environment smart

Wear breathable, loose, natural-fiber clothing. Air-conditioned spaces help. If you swim in a chlorinated pool, rinse off immediately and moisturize.

💡 Even during a flare, you don't have to stop moving. Adjust your activity — don't abandon it.

Thinking it Through

Question 3 of 4
Your eczema is currently flaring on your arms. You normally do high-intensity interval training three times a week. Which approach is best?
Exactly. You don't have to stop moving — you just adjust. Lower-sweat activities reduce irritation, lukewarm water is gentler than hot, and the 3-minute moisturizing window applies here just as much as it does to the Soak and Seal method from Module 2.

Thinking it Through

Question 4 of 4
You've been under significant stress for two weeks. From your tracking, you know a flare usually follows two to three days after a stressful period. Right now your skin looks fine. What's the most proactive step?
Great thinking. Proactive care — acting before a flare fully develops — is one of the most important skills in eczema self-management. You're using your tracking data to predict and prevent, not just react. This is exactly what having an Eczema Action Plan with a step-up strategy makes possible.
