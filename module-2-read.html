What is Itch?

Understanding the biology helps you take it less personally.

Itch (or pruritus) is a sensation produced by nerve signals in your skin. In eczema, the skin barrier is damaged, making it much easier for irritants to trigger those nerves — resulting in itch that is often more intense and harder to ignore than in healthy skin.
The itch of eczema isn't only about your skin. Your nervous system, immune system, and even your emotional state are all involved. That's why itch often feels worse when you're tired, stressed, or distracted.
The urge to scratch is real and powerful — it's wired into your biology. Understanding why it happens is the first step towards managing it.

Emotional Triggers

Your inner state affects your skin more than most people realise.

Stress

Work pressure, difficult relationships, or life changes can activate your body's itch response — even before a visible flare appears.

Anxiety

Worry and anxious thoughts keep your nervous system on high alert, making itch signals harder to ignore and easier to act on.

Tiredness

Fatigue reduces your ability to resist the urge to scratch and makes itch feel more intense and overwhelming.

Low Mood

When you're feeling down, itch can feel harder to ignore and harder to cope with — and the cycle becomes harder to break.

Physical Triggers

Knowing these can help you reduce or plan around them.

Heat & Sweating

Sweat irritates the damaged skin barrier, and warmth intensifies nerve itch signals.

Rough Fabrics

Wool, synthetic fibres, or tight clothing can directly abrade already-sensitive skin.

Fragrances & Soaps

Products with perfume, alcohol, or harsh detergents strip the skin barrier.

Dust & Pet Dander

Environmental allergens can trigger immune responses that worsen itch and inflammation.

Dry Skin

When the skin barrier dries out, it becomes more permeable and more susceptible to itch signals.

Why Itch Gets Worse at Night

Four reasons the darkness makes itch harder to handle.

Fewer Distractions

Without the day's activity to occupy your mind, itch signals get your full, undivided attention.

Cortisol Dips

Your body's natural anti-inflammatory cortisol drops overnight, reducing its suppression of itch.

Body Temperature

Bed warmth raises your core temperature, which intensifies nerve itch signals in the skin.

Skin Dryness

Skin loses moisture overnight — especially in heated bedrooms — worsening the itch barrier breakdown.

Habit Reversal Therapy (HRT)

Teaching your hands a different response.

01
Notice the Urge

Build awareness of the moment the scratching urge arises — before your hand moves automatically. Notice the sensation without immediately acting on it.

02
Use a Competing Response

Replace scratching with a deliberate physical action: clench your fist tightly, press your palm flat against your thigh, or apply firm pressure with your fingertips on the itch site.

03
Practise Consistently

HRT works best when practised repeatedly. Over time, the competing response becomes your new automatic reaction — and the scratching habit weakens.

Soak & Seal — and Wet Wraps

Moisture locked in quickly makes a measurable difference.

1
Warm bath or shower (5–10 min)

Warm — not hot. Hot water strips the skin barrier and worsens inflammation.

2
Pat skin gently — leave slightly damp

Don't rub dry. Damp skin absorbs moisturizer more effectively than completely dry skin.

3
Apply thick moisturizer within 3 minutes

Use a cream or ointment — not a lotion. Lotions are too watery for eczema and evaporate quickly.

Wet wrap therapy Apply a layer of medicated cream or moisturizer, then wrap the skin in damp cotton, followed by a dry layer over the top. It can provide significant relief during severe flares. Ask your doctor before trying it at home.

Protecting Your Sleep

Small changes at bedtime can significantly reduce night scratching.

🌡️

Keep your room coolA cooler bedroom (around 16–18°C) reduces the body temperature rise that intensifies itch at night.

🧤

Wear soft cotton glovesCotton gloves worn to bed significantly limit the damage done by unconscious sleep scratching.

✂️

Keep nails short and smoothShort nails cause far less skin damage when scratching occurs during sleep.

🛁

Soak & Seal before bedA moisturizing routine before sleep locks in hydration and reduces overnight dryness and itch.

Thinking it Through

Question 1 of 4
You scratch intensely during an eczema flare and feel brief relief. But within minutes, the itch returns stronger than before, and your skin is now red and raw. This is an example of:
Exactly. Scratching gives momentary relief by overloading the nerve signals — but it also damages the skin barrier, triggering more inflammation and more itch. Breaking this cycle is the core aim of this module.

Thinking it Through

Question 2 of 4
It's been a particularly stressful week at work and your eczema has flared significantly — even though you haven't changed your moisturizer or been exposed to any new products. Which of the following best explains this?
That's right. Stress activates the body's fight-or-flight response, releasing hormones that fuel skin inflammation and heighten the nervous system's sensitivity to itch — even when there's no new physical trigger.

Thinking it Through

Question 3 of 4
Your dermatologist suggests that whenever you feel the urge to scratch, you clench your fist tightly for 30 seconds instead. Over time, this becomes your automatic response to the itch urge. What technique is this called?
Correct. Habit Reversal Therapy teaches you to replace the automatic scratching response with a competing response — a deliberate physical action that interrupts the habit loop and gives your skin time to recover.

Thinking it Through

Question 4 of 4
You've been waking up at 3am to find you've been scratching your arms badly in your sleep. Which of the following approaches is most likely to help?
That's the right idea. Soak & Seal locks in moisture before the skin dries out overnight, and soft cotton gloves reduce the damage from sleep scratching. Hot showers, fans on dry skin, and skipping moisturizer would all make things worse.

Let's Recap! Key Takeaways

  • › Itch leads to scratching. Scratching damages skin. Damaged skin itches more. This is the cycle you are breaking.
  • › Emotional triggers — stress, anxiety, tiredness — are just as real as physical ones.
  • › Itch gets worse at night because of fewer distractions, dropping cortisol, warmth, and skin dryness.
  • › Habit Reversal Therapy gives your hands a different, deliberate response to the urge to scratch.
  • › Soak & Seal — within 3 minutes of bathing — is one of the most effective ways to lock in moisture and reduce itch.
